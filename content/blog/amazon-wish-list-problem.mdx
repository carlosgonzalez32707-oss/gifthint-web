Amazon wish lists have been around since 1999. In the same 25 years, smartphones were invented, streaming replaced DVDs, and social media rose and mostly fell. Amazon wish lists got a slightly cleaner interface.

They still have the same fundamental problems they had when the feature launched. And because Amazon wish lists are the default — the thing everyone reaches for first — those problems keep playing out every year, around every birthday and Christmas.

Here are the three issues that pushed us to build GiftHint, and what we did differently.

## Problem 1: They Lock You Into One Store

This is the obvious one, but it's worth spelling out because the constraint is genuinely limiting.

Amazon sells a lot. But it doesn't sell *everything* you want. The handmade ceramic mug from a maker on Etsy. The specific face oil from a brand that doesn't stock on Amazon. The cookbook from an independent press. The running shoes that are $40 cheaper at a specialist retailer than the Amazon listing.

When you build a wishlist on Amazon, you're implicitly telling your friends and family: only things from this one store. That's not a reflection of how people actually shop — it's an artifact of using a wishlist tool tied to a single retailer.

Most people work around this with a Google Doc or a Notes screenshot shared by message. That creates a new problem: there's no way to mark items as claimed, and duplicate gifts become inevitable.

## Problem 2: The Link Is Ugly and Confusing

Go to your Amazon wishlist right now and find the shareable link.

It looks something like this:

```
https://www.amazon.com/hz/wishlist/ls/3XXXXXXXXXXXXXX?ref_=wl_share
```

That URL communicates nothing — not whose list it is, not what occasion it's for, not whether it's worth clicking. It looks like a system reference number, not a personal page. When you paste it into a WhatsApp group, some people will click. Others will ignore it and buy something random anyway — because a URL that looks like that doesn't inspire confidence.

Compare that to `gifthint.io/list/yourname`. Immediately clear, immediately trustworthy, immediately usable. That difference in presentation affects how many people actually click through and buy.

## Problem 3: Privacy Works Backwards

Amazon wishlists are set to **public by default**.

Public as in: anyone on the internet can find and view your wishlist if they search for your name or Amazon account. Most people don't know this. They set up a list, share it with family, and assume it's private — but it's technically accessible to anyone who finds your account.

There's a second privacy issue that affects the experience from the inside: Amazon shows *you* a "Purchased" status alongside items on your own list, sometimes revealing who bought something. For birthday and Christmas gifts, this spoils the surprise. You know what your partner ordered. You know what your mum chose.

GiftHint works in the opposite direction. Lists are private by default — you have to actively share the link for anyone to see it. When someone buys something, it's marked "claimed" *for other viewers* — but the list owner never sees who bought what, or even that a purchase happened at all. The surprise is structurally protected.

## What We Built Instead

GiftHint started from a simple question: what would a wishlist look like if you designed it today, for how people actually shop?

**Save from any store.** A browser extension places a floating heart on every product page across the internet. One tap saves the item to your list — regardless of whether it's from Amazon, Etsy, Selfridges, IKEA, or a small brand's own website. If a product has a price, GiftHint can save it.

**One clean link.** Your list lives at `gifthint.io/list/yourname`. It's shareable anywhere — a text message, a birthday event description, an Instagram bio, a family WhatsApp. People click it and see your items without creating an account or logging into anything.

**Claiming that actually works.** When a friend buys something from your list, that item is automatically marked as claimed for everyone else who visits the link. No coordination required. No duplicate gifts. No awkward conversation about who's returning what.

The extension takes about 30 seconds to install. Saving your first item takes about ten seconds. Your shareable link is ready immediately.
